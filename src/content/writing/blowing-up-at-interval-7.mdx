"You're going too fast."

I've heard that from my coach more times than I can count, and for a long time I nodded without listening. I'd been running for two or three years and thought I knew how to train. There was some arrogance in that.

Intervals in a group pull you along. You see people ahead pushing, and a few reps in, already tired, you push anyway. Then you reach interval 7 of 12 and something gives. "Right, guys, I'm done for today." On your way out the coach calls over: "Finish the workout. Go slower, but finish it." So, out of pride, you finish it, with nothing left, and you pay for it the next day.

Eventually I had to admit my coach was right. I wasn't short of effort. I was putting too much of it in the wrong places. And I couldn't have told you what heart rate or pace "threshold" or "zone 2" meant for me. So I gave my numbers to Claude.

## The two numbers

<Chart wide={lactateWide} narrow={lactateNarrow} title="How lactate climbs" caption="Flat when you run easy, steep past LT2, and training moves both to faster paces. The typical shape, not my lab data." />

**LT1** is the pace or heart rate where the lactate in your blood starts to rise. Below it you're running easy. Zone 2 lives there, and so should most of your running.

**LT2** is the highest intensity where your body still clears lactate as fast as it makes it. Above it, lactate and fatigue pile up fast: you have minutes left, not an hour. When a coach says "threshold", this is the line.

Train well and both lines move to faster paces.

## What the AI gave me

It worked from two runs: my last 10K race and a 5K time trial. The number that mattered most was LT2 at 180 bpm, about 4:20-4:30 per km. The rest is in the chart. These are estimates from watch data, not a lab test, but for the first time I had numbers to aim at.

## 19 May, looked at again

Then I pulled an old session off my watch: 19 May, 12 x 800 m at 10K pace minus 10 seconds, so 4:16 per km, with a minute's rest. For a session that long you ramp up, and I did: the first two reps at 4:48 and 4:43. What I missed is that the ramp-up was already the answer. Reps that easy should sit well under LT2, and by the second one my heart rate was at 179, where my 10K sits. Rep 7 broke at 5:28.

It was a pace workout, but my heart rate was telling me it was a bad day, and I didn't listen. A coach would have called it at rep 2: forget 4:16, run the rest by heart rate. One rep at LT1, 5:30-5:40, to bring it down, then pick up to whatever keeps each rep under 178. That day, that was about 5:10: at 4:40 I was 8 beats higher than the week before.

## What changed

I tried the numbers first in a fartlek, and the target paces were faster than I expected. You have to trust them and settle in.

Then, on Tuesday 15 September, I ran 2-minute reps at 4:40-4:48 per km with my heart rate at 171-177, touching 180 in the shorter pieces between them. I wasn't afraid of it, because I knew my LT2 was 180. One beat over and the session falls apart. Just under it is hard, but I can hold it.

That's the real gain. Your coach says "threshold" or "easy", and now it's a number on your watch, not a guess.

It was a better session. Not because I pushed harder, but because I used exactly what my body had that day. Nothing held back, and none of the extra that used to finish me at interval 7.

## Do this

1. Go to Garmin Connect on the web and download a recent 5K or 10K. It has to be recent: give the AI old data and it describes a runner you no longer are.
2. Give it to an AI with this prompt:

```text
Take a look at this 5K or 10K run.
Work out my LT1, my LT2 and my zone 2.
```

3. No recent race? Sign up for a 10K, or run one all out on your own.
4. Then turn every session your coach gives you into a heart rate or a pace on your watch, with a back-off line a couple of beats under LT2.
