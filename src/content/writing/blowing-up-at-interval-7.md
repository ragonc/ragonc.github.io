"You're going too fast. You went out too hard."

I've heard that from my coach more times than I can count, and for a long time I nodded without really listening. I'd been running for two or three years. I thought I knew how to train. If I'm honest, there was some arrogance in that.

Sometimes I took the hint and started a little slower. But intervals in a group pull you along. You want to stay with the others. You see people ahead pushing, and a few reps in, when you can already feel the fatigue, you push anyway. Then you push some more.

Then you reach interval 7 of 10 and something gives. You gather your courage and say it: "Right, guys, I'm done for today. I'm heading home."

On your way out, your coach, who is running the session with you, calls over: "Finish the workout. Go slower, but finish it."

So, as much out of pride as anything, you turn round and finish it. Exhausted, with everything you have left.

The next day you train again. Legs, maybe, or another session. And the weights that felt easy last week feel heavier.

## The uncomfortable conclusion

After enough of those sessions I had to take seriously what my coach had been saying all along: I was training in the wrong zones. I wasn't short of effort. I was putting too much of it in the wrong places.

The trouble was that I didn't know where my zones were. I'd heard "threshold", "zone 2" and "VO2max" a hundred times, but I couldn't have told you what heart rate or pace any of them meant for me.

So I decided to put my numbers into Claude.

## What I actually did

Nothing sophisticated. I went to my Garmin's page on the web, downloaded the file for a recent run, and gave it to the AI with a very simple prompt:

> Take a look at this 5K or 10K run.
> Work out my LT1, my LT2 and my zone 2.

A 5K or a 10K both work. What matters is that you've run it recently. I went a step further and gave it both my runs and my structured workouts.

It put my zone 2 at 6:30-6:50 per km, my LT1 at about 152 bpm and 5:30-5:40 per km, and my LT2 at 180 bpm and 4:20-4:30 per km. From the same data, my half marathon comes out at about 4:38 per km, 1:37 to 1:38. These are estimates from watch data, not a lab test, but for the first time I had numbers to aim at.

## The two numbers that matter

![The classic lactate curve: flat at easy speeds, rising from LT1 (mine: 5:30-5:40/km, 152 bpm), climbing steeply after LT2 (mine: 4:20-4:30/km, 180 bpm). A dashed curve shows the same shape after training, shifted to faster speeds.](/images/lactate-curve.svg)

**LT1**, your first lactate threshold, is the pace or heart rate at which the lactate in your blood starts to rise above its resting level. Below it you are running easy. Your zone 2 sits there, and so should most of your running.

**LT2**, your second threshold, is the highest intensity at which your body can still clear lactate as fast as it produces it. Above it, lactate builds up quickly, and so does the fatigue: you have minutes left, not an hour. When a coach says "run this at threshold", this is the line they mean.

I tested the numbers almost straight away, in a fartlek run, and the AI was right. It isn't easy at first. The pace it gives you may be faster than you expect, or slower, and you have to trust it and settle in. After a while, though, training gets simpler, because you know exactly where you need to be.

That's the real gain. When someone tells you "this one's at threshold", or "this is VO2max, not threshold", or "this has to be easy", you have a number on your watch to hold on to, heart rate or pace, instead of guessing at an effort. The workouts your coach gives you, and the ones you pick yourself, start to make sense.

## What changed in a session

In a workout last week I held 178, 179, 180 bpm and wasn't afraid of it, because I knew my LT2 was around 180. Sitting just below it is hard, but I can sustain it. Above 180 I'd have been over LT2, and that's where I have to be careful, because that's where a session falls apart.

What surprised me was the pace. At that heart rate I was running faster than I thought I could at that effort.

It was a better session. Not because I pushed harder, but because I used exactly what my body could give me that day. No less than that, and none of the extra that used to leave me ready to quit at interval 7.

## My advice

Take your most recent run, a 5K or a 10K, and give it to an AI with the prompt above. It has to be recent. Give it old data and everything it tells you describes a runner you no longer are.

If you don't have a recent one, sign up for a 10K race, or run one all out on your own, and use it as your reference point. Once you have a 10K to anchor to, turning every session your coach gives you into a heart rate or a pace becomes far simpler.
